Alaska voters keep ranked-choice voting and cities in California and Colorado adopt it

On Nov. 3, Alaskans turned down a repeal of their ranked-choice voting (RCV) system, while two cities chose to bring RCV into their local races.

Alaska Ballot Measure 2 would have restored party primaries and single-choice voting and dropped some campaign finance disclosure rules. It failed 53.6% to 46.4%.

Alaska’s system sends the top four finishers of a single, all-party primary to a ranked general election. If nobody tops 50%+1, last-place candidates are eliminated and their ballots reassigned until two remain.

Voters approved it 50.6% to 49.5% in 2020 and kept it in 2024, 50.1% to 49.9%.

Unofficial results as of Nov. 16 show 37 of 53 RCV contests on Nov. 3 ended in round one. The U.S. Senate and at-large U.S. House races needed a second round, while Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins (D) won the governor’s race with 50.9% of first choices. Round one also settled six of 10 state Senate and 30 of 40 state House races.

Alaska and Maine are the only states using RCV for regular federal or statewide races. Between 2002 and 2024, voters in eight states decided 12 statewide RCV measures, approving four of 10 adoption measures and one of two repeal or preemption measures.

Seven states now authorize or require RCV in some elections; 19 ban it statewide.

Locally, Irvine’s Measure D passed 53.3% to 46.7%, bringing RCV to mayor and council races after Jan. 1, 2028, where feasible, and Longmont’s Question 3C passed 56% to 44%, starting in November 2029. Honolulu rejected RCV 55.4% to 44.6%. Nationwide, 42 localities in 14 states plus Washington, D.C., use or plan to use it.

The three local RCV measures in 2026 matched 2024 but trailed each of the previous five years; 2022’s nine were the most since 1966.

Ninth Circuit rules against parts of California climate disclosure requirements

On Nov. 9, the Ninth Circuit held that parts of California’s corporate climate disclosure laws likely violate the First Amendment, partly reversing a lower court’s refusal to grant business groups a preliminary injunction.

The court left SB 253’s Scope 1 and Scope 2 reporting in place (emissions from a company’s own sources and purchased energy) but reversed on Scope 3 value-chain disclosures and SB 261’s climate-risk reports.

  • SB 253 (2023): companies doing business in California with over $1 billion in annual revenue must report emissions.
  • SB 261 (2023): those with over $500 million must report climate-related financial risks every two years.

The panel treated Scope 1 and 2 as commercial speech that survives intermediate scrutiny, while Scope 3 and SB 261 compel noncommercial speech and fail strict scrutiny. The Nov. 10 deadline for first Scope 1 and 2 reports still applies.

The district court will now consider whether Scope 3 can be severed from SB 253; the ruling is not a final judgment. In November 2025, the Ninth Circuit had paused SB 261 during the appeal.

The candidates in this Austin, Texas, City Council runoff were 70 votes apart on Nov. 3, here’s a closer look at their campaigns

Amber Goodwin (34.3%, 10,191 votes) and Misael Ramos (34.1%, 10,121 votes) led six candidates for the open District 1 seat on the Austin, Texas, City Council and meet in a Dec. 12 runoff.

Goodwin, a Travis County assistant district attorney, is endorsed by U.S. Reps. Greg Casar (D) and Joaquin Castro (D), three council members and several unions. Ramos, a former business analyst and union activist, is endorsed by Democratic Socialists of America chapters, Progressive Victory, CAIR Action TX, the Austin Sierra Club and a South Austin neighborhood coalition.

In Ballotpedia’s Candidate Connection survey, Goodwin proposed an economic opportunity fund, a community health hub and an eviction prevention and safety initiative; Ramos stressed housing affordability, infrastructure upgrades and more responsive government.

Five of 10 council seats were up in 2026. Districts 1, 3 and 5 went to runoffs, Selena Xie won District 8 and incumbent Zohaib Qadri won District 9.
