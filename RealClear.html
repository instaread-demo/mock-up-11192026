Department of Government Inefficiency: Can Vance Reset Anti-Fraud Push?

By James Varney, RealClearInvestigations
November 17, 2026

President Trump’s decision to remove JD Vance from negotiations with Iran may be raising questions about the vice president’s political future, but some good government groups are hailing Trump’s decision to have Vance focus on his position as “fraud czar.”

Trump has made cracking down on waste and abuse one of his signature policies. But the administration’s anti-fraud efforts – including the Department of Government Efficiency, created in 2025, and the Task Force to Eliminate Fraud, established in March – have been marked by poorly executed directives and difficult-to-verify claims.

Matt Weidinger of the American Enterprise Institute said the push has so far amounted to “scattered press conferences” rather than a central focus.

AEI's Matt Weidinger says anti-fraud efforts so far leave plenty of room for improvement.
AEI

Watchdogs hope Vance’s narrower portfolio brings a reset, while noting that fraud and waste have resisted decades of piecemeal efforts.

The Government Accountability Office puts yearly fraud losses at $233 billion to $521 billion, using fiscal 2018–2022 data, and agencies estimated $186 billion in improper payments, not all of them fraud, in fiscal 2025.

Experts told RealClearInvestigations little of that money comes back, citing costly prosecutions, fleeing perpetrators, poor coordination and reluctance to chase innocently overpaid citizens.

Success could help Vance after the midterm defeats; failure could hurt him as the “border czar” role hurt Kamala Harris.

Jeremy Portnoy of OpenTheBooks praised the task force for keeping fraud in public view. Weidinger told RCI the role has not yet become the burden some predicted.

Big guns, scattered shots

After launching DOGE under Elon Musk in 2025, Trump put Vance in charge of the anti-fraud drive last spring. The task force, spanning every Cabinet department but Defense, then stalled amid Democratic resistance and internal inefficiency.

President Trump has rolled out some of his biggest guns, including Elon Musk, to combat fraud, waste and abuse.
AP

The March executive order set 30-day and 60-day deadlines for agencies to flag vulnerable transactions and propose safeguards. Officials told RCI all were met, but no plans have been released some six months later.

Weidinger and others said that makes progress hard to judge, unlike the bureaucracy’s quick response to President Biden’s push on diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives. Tom Schatz of Citizens Against Government Waste said the public deserves to be told.

Some figures are also in doubt. In June, Education Secretary Linda McMahon credited her department with preventing more than $1 billion in student aid fraud after a House vote targeting “ghost students”; Portnoy said no one could supply supporting data.

A House Energy and Commerce report quoted HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. blaming the Biden administration for cutting oversight as spending neared $2.2 trillion a year. Kennedy testified in April that all but six of 80 integrity staff were fired, but neither the committee nor HHS could confirm it for RCI.

HHS could not verify Secretary Robert Kennedy Jr.'s claim that the Biden administration had gutted the department' program integrity office.
AP

CMS’s site still shows a Center for Program Integrity with a $1 billion budget and over 500 staff; its deputy, Jeneen Iwugo, did not reply to RCI.

Fraud experts, including Jeremy Nighohossian of the Competitive Enterprise Institute, said Biden-era oversight did slacken, but the current administration’s numbers often go unsupported.

More fuzzy math

The task force’s Fraud Ledger showed $391.6 billion in fraud “uncovered,” “stopped,” or “enforced” as of Nov. 17, counting 13 months that predate the task force. Weidinger warned many such figures are mere estimates.

“Stopped” includes $72 billion in projected yearly savings that a later administration could undo. The “enforced” tally slipped from $59.1 billion in a Sept. 27 New York Times fact check to $58.9 billion by Nov. 17.

The SBA inspector general put pandemic loan fraud at $200 billion of $1.2 trillion; the ledger credits the SBA with $146.8 billion, the most of any agency.

Recovery blues

Recoveries stay small, partly because foreign gangs commit much of the theft; Weidinger said overseas actors were behind an estimated 70 percent of pandemic jobless-benefit fraud.

Tom Schatz, president of Citizens Against Government Waste, says recovering stolen money is difficult.
CAGW

Schatz added that some COVID-era statutes of limitations have lapsed and Congress curtailed Recovery Audit Contractors.

CMS has since sought industry ideas and opened more data to AI-equipped citizen sleuths. The SBA added fraud hotlines, and the Justice Department created “FOCUS” teams in May and a National Fraud Detection Center in August.

Citizen sleuths

Investigators told RCI that officials are still sifting weak tips, and cases take time and money. Improper payments, which GAO tallies at some $3 trillion since fiscal year 2003, rose $24 billion to $186 billion in FY2025.

Experts say the real goal is replacing “pay and chase” with up-front checks.

White House officials conceded many steps are temporary and faulted a reluctant Congress. Nighohossian pointed to tighter Medicaid eligibility checks in the “One Big Beautiful Bill,” the committee said CMS agreed with all 37 of its April recommendations, and Vance held an August roundtable, but lame-duck action looks unlikely.

Task Force officials called bureaucratic inertia the larger hurdle and said this administration is the first to tackle it seriously.

President Trump's spotlight on wasteful spending has inspired a coterie of fraud-hunting citizen sleuths, including Nick Shirley (left).
AP

States run much of Medicaid and Medicare, so officials want closer cooperation; the House report faulted reactive oversight for heavy annual losses.

Schatz, a Hill veteran since 1986, said no sustained reform effort has ever taken hold, and even a durable anti-fraud network among agencies and law enforcement would help. He said the cause should be bipartisan.

Whether Vance’s renewed focus yields the lasting change watchdogs want remains an open question.
